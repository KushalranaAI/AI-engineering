# Speculative decoding, disaggregation and reading the research

Two advanced techniques, both built on the prefill/decode split from 12.1, and a habit that keeps you current once this level is out of date.

## Speculative decoding

Decode reads all the weights to produce one token, while the compute units sit mostly idle. Checking five tokens in one forward pass costs almost the same as producing one, because verifying k tokens is a small prefill. Speculative decoding exploits that:

1. A cheap **drafter** proposes k tokens.
2. The big **target** model runs once over all k positions.
3. Accept the longest prefix of draft tokens the target agrees with, plus one token from the target itself. With the standard rejection-sampling rule the output distribution is exactly the target's, so quality is unchanged.

Drafters, from simplest to strongest:

- **N-gram / prompt lookup:** copy candidate continuations from the prompt itself. Free, and very effective when output repeats input (code editing, extraction, RAG answers quoting sources).
- **A small model from the same family** (for example a 1B drafting for a 70B, sharing the tokenizer).
- **Trained heads on the target** (Medusa, EAGLE and successors) that predict several future tokens from the target's own hidden states; usually the best acceptance per unit of cost.

How much it helps depends on the **acceptance rate** α (the chance each draft token is accepted). With k draft tokens, the expected number of tokens per target pass is (1 − α^(k+1)) / (1 − α). At α = 0.7 and k = 4 that is about 2.8 tokens per pass; at α = 0.4 it is about 1.6, and the drafter's cost may eat the gain.

In vLLM it is configured with a JSON argument, for example:

```bash
vllm serve meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \
  --speculative-config '{"method": "ngram", "num_speculative_tokens": 4, "prompt_lookup_max": 4}'
```

EAGLE-style and draft-model configs use the same flag with a draft model path; check the speculative decoding page for your version, as the options change often. Watch the acceptance metrics the engine exports.

### Where it stops helping

- **High load.** At large batch sizes decode is already closer to compute-bound; the "free" spare compute is gone, and verifying rejected drafts wastes it. Speculation shines at low-to-medium concurrency and latency-sensitive traffic, and can *reduce* throughput at saturation. Some engines disable it dynamically above a batch size threshold.
- **Low acceptance.** Creative writing at high temperature, or a drafter trained on different data.
- **Expensive drafters.** A drafter that is not much smaller than the target.

So benchmark it at your real concurrency, not batch 1, and report both ITL and throughput.

## Disaggregated prefill and decode

On one GPU, prefill (compute-bound, bursty) and decode (memory-bound, steady) fight over the same steps. Chunked prefill (12.5) softens this. **Disaggregation** separates them: a pool of prefill workers computes the prompt's KV cache, transfers it to a pool of decode workers, and those generate the tokens.

Why bother: each pool can be sized and tuned for its own bottleneck (different tensor parallel sizes, batch limits, even GPU types), prefill bursts no longer cause decode ITL spikes, and you can set TTFT and ITL targets independently. Papers such as DistServe and Splitwise showed large goodput gains under strict latency SLOs, and production systems (Mooncake at Moonshot, NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d, SGLang's and vLLM's disaggregation modes via KV connectors) build on this.

The cost is moving the KV cache. A 2,000-token prompt on an 8B model is 2,000 × 128 KiB ≈ 256 MB. Over a fast RDMA fabric at roughly 50 GB/s that is about 5 ms; over 25 Gb/s Ethernet it is roughly 80 ms, which may exceed the prefill itself. Disaggregation pays off at scale, with fast interconnects, long prompts and strict latency targets. For a single node and modest traffic, chunked prefill is simpler and usually enough.

## Reading inference research

Model launches tell you which weights exist. Inference research and engine changelogs tell you how to serve them, and that is where your service gets faster. A sustainable habit:

- **Engine release notes**, monthly: vLLM, SGLang, llama.cpp, TensorRT-LLM. Note defaults that changed (prefix caching on, chunked prefill on, new KV layouts), because they change your benchmark baseline.
- **Papers**, a few per month from systems venues (OSDI, SOSP, MLSys, EuroSys) and arXiv. Read the abstract, the evaluation setup and the baseline before the headline number: "3× faster than vLLM" against an old version with default flags is a different claim from 3× against a tuned one.
- **Design docs and RFCs** in the engine repos' issues, where trade-offs are argued in the open.
- **Reproduce one claim per quarter** on your own hardware and traffic. You will learn more from one disagreement than from ten summaries.

Keep a short log: claim, source, conditions, your result.

## Try it

Enable n-gram speculative decoding on your server. Benchmark two workloads, "rewrite this 400-word passage fixing typos" and "write a short story", at concurrency 1, 32 and 256, measuring ITL p50 and output throughput with and without speculation.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

The rewrite task has high acceptance because output copies input, so ITL should improve clearly at concurrency 1 and 32. The story task has low acceptance and gains little. At 256, gains shrink or turn negative on both, because the batch is no longer leaving compute idle.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why does speculative decoding not change output quality?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The target model verifies every token, and the acceptance rule (rejection sampling) is designed so the final distribution matches what the target alone would have produced.
</details>

2. Why does speculation help less at high concurrency?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It spends spare compute to verify extra tokens. At large batch sizes that spare compute is already used, and rejected drafts become pure waste.
</details>

3. What is the main cost of disaggregated serving, and when is it worth paying?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Transferring the KV cache from prefill to decode workers (plus operating two pools). It pays off at scale with fast interconnects, long prompts and strict, separate TTFT and ITL targets.
</details>
