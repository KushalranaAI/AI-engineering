# Quantization trade-offs

Quantization stores numbers with fewer bits. Since decode is bound by bytes read (12.1), halving the bytes per weight can nearly double single-stream decode speed and frees memory for KV cache. But "4-bit is four times faster" is wrong often enough to cost you, and every format changes the model's answers a little. This module is about choosing with numbers.

## What can be quantized

| Target | Saves | Speeds up |
|---|---|---|
| **Weights only** (W8A16, W4A16) | Weight memory | Memory-bound decode, mostly at small and medium batch |
| **Weights and activations** (W8A8 in FP8 or INT8) | Weight memory | Decode *and* compute-bound prefill, because the matrix multiply itself runs in 8-bit on tensor cores |
| **KV cache** (FP8 KV) | KV memory, so more or longer sequences | Attention reads at long context |

Weight-only formats dequantize weights back to 16-bit inside the kernel, so the multiply still runs at 16-bit speed. That's fine when you are waiting on memory, and a cost when you are not: at large batch sizes, a W4A16 model can be no faster, or even slower, than an FP8 one.

## The formats

**FP8** (E4M3 for weights and activations) is the default first choice on GPUs with FP8 tensor cores (NVIDIA Hopper, Ada and newer). With per-channel or per-tensor scales, quality loss on most instruction-tuned models is small, memory halves, and both prefill and decode get faster. In vLLM, `--quantization fp8` quantizes a BF16 checkpoint on the fly at load time; pre-quantized FP8 checkpoints are detected automatically. On older GPUs without FP8 hardware, engines may fall back to weight-only FP8 kernels, which save memory but speed up less.

**INT8** (W8A8 with methods such as SmoothQuant, or weight-only) runs on INT8 tensor cores, which older generations such as Ampere have. Activations have outlier channels that are hard to fit in 8-bit integers; SmoothQuant moves part of that difficulty into the weights.

**INT4** weight-only halves memory again: an 8B model drops from about 16 GB to roughly 5 GB including group scales. This is what makes a 70B model fit on one 48 GB card, or an 8B model leave plenty of KV room on a 24 GB one. Quality loss is larger and depends heavily on how the weights were quantized:

- **GPTQ** quantizes one layer at a time, column by column, and uses second-order information from a small calibration set to adjust the not-yet-quantized weights so they compensate for the rounding error.
- **AWQ** observes that a small fraction of weight channels matter far more, identified by the size of the *activations* they multiply. It scales those channels up before quantizing so they lose less precision, with no backpropagation needed.

Both need a calibration set; one that resembles your traffic (code, another language) can matter. Newer GPUs add 4-bit floating-point formats (for example NVFP4 and MXFP4 on NVIDIA's Blackwell generation); the same trade-off thinking applies.

Rough memory for an 8B model:

| Format | Weights | Relative decode speed (memory-bound, small batch) |
|---|---|---|
| BF16 | ~16 GB | 1× |
| FP8 / INT8 | ~8 GB | up to ~1.8× |
| INT4 (AWQ/GPTQ) | ~5 GB | up to ~2.5-3× |

These are ceilings from the byte count; kernels, batch size and GPU decide what you actually get. Measure.

## Where checkpoints come from

Prefer quantized checkpoints from the model publisher or well-known quantization projects, and read how they were made (method, group size, calibration data). Or make your own with a tool such as `llm-compressor` (from the vLLM project) or AutoAWQ/GPTQ tooling, which also lets you pick a calibration set that matches your data. Check each tool's current docs; this area changes fast.

## Measure quality, not just speed

Public perplexity numbers are averages over generic text. Your product might be the exception: long-context retrieval, arithmetic, code, a minority language and strict JSON output are typical places where low-bit models fail first. For each variant run:

1. Your task eval set from Level 7 (same prompts, greedy decoding), with the score difference and confidence interval.
2. A few spot checks on known-fragile cases: long input, numbers, structured output.
3. The load test from 12.4 at your target concurrency, not just at batch 1.

Then fill one table: format, weight GB, KV capacity, p50/p95 TTFT and ITL, throughput at SLO, eval score. Pick the cheapest row whose eval score you can live with.

## Try it

Serve the same 7-8B model in BF16, FP8 and an AWQ INT4 checkpoint. For each, record weight memory and KV capacity from the startup log, single-stream decode tokens/s, throughput at 256 concurrent requests, and score on 50 questions from your eval set.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Expect memory to drop roughly 2× and 3× and KV capacity to rise accordingly. Single-stream speed improves most for INT4. At 256 concurrency, FP8 often matches or beats INT4 on GPUs with FP8 tensor cores, because the batch has moved towards compute-bound and weight-only kernels pay a dequantization cost. Eval scores usually drop little for FP8 and somewhat more for INT4, with the biggest losses on your hardest category.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why might INT4 weight-only quantization not speed up a heavily loaded server?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

At large batch sizes decode moves towards compute-bound, and weight-only kernels still multiply in 16-bit after dequantizing, which adds work. The memory saving no longer translates into speed.
</details>

2. What is the main difference in how AWQ and GPTQ protect accuracy?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

AWQ scales up the weight channels tied to large activations so they lose less precision; GPTQ quantizes column by column and updates the remaining weights to compensate for rounding error using second-order information.
</details>

3. Why evaluate on your own tasks instead of trusting published perplexity?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Quantization damage is uneven: it can hit specific skills (long context, maths, code, structured output, certain languages) that average perplexity barely reflects.
</details>
