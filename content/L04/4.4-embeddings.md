# Embeddings

Keyword search needs the query and the document to share words. People don't cooperate: they ask "can I work from Spain for a month?" when the policy says "temporary remote work abroad". An **embedding** turns text into a vector of numbers so that texts with similar meaning end up near each other, which lets you search by meaning instead of spelling.

## What a vector captures

An embedding model reads a text and outputs a fixed-length list of floats, for example 768 or 1,536 of them. The individual numbers mean nothing on their own; what matters is distance. The models are trained on huge sets of matching pairs (questions and answers, titles and pages, paraphrases), pulled together during training, while unrelated texts are pushed apart. The result is a space where "remote work abroad" and "work from Spain" sit close together.

What it captures well: topic, paraphrase, intent, cross-lingual meaning (for multilingual models). What it captures poorly: exact identifiers, numbers, negation ("refunds are **not** available" sits close to "refunds are available") and anything rare in training data.

## Embedding documents and queries

You embed both sides with the same model:

```python
from openai import OpenAI
import numpy as np

client = OpenAI()
MODEL = "text-embedding-3-small"  # example model

def embed(texts: list[str]) -> np.ndarray:
    resp = client.embeddings.create(model=MODEL, input=texts)
    return np.array([d.embedding for d in resp.data], dtype=np.float32)

chunks = ["Employees may work remotely from abroad for up to 30 days.",
          "Expense claims must be filed within 60 days."]
doc_vecs = embed(chunks)
query_vec = embed(["Can I work from Spain for a month?"])[0]
print(doc_vecs @ query_vec)   # first score should be higher
```

Batch your document calls (many texts per request) and cache results by a hash of the text: re-embedding unchanged chunks is pure waste.

Some models are **asymmetric**: they expect a hint saying whether a text is a query or a passage. Open models such as the E5 family use prefixes like `"query: "` and `"passage: "`; some hosted APIs take a parameter such as `input_type` or a task type. Using the wrong mode, or none, quietly lowers recall. Read the model card.

## Choosing a model

Public leaderboards such as MTEB are a useful shortlist, not a decision. Compare candidates on your own data:

| Factor | What to check |
|--------|---------------|
| Quality | Hit rate on 30-50 of your real questions with known answers |
| Languages and domain | Multilingual? Code? Legal or medical terms? |
| Max input | Longer than your chunks, or text is cut off |
| Dimensions | Storage and search cost scale with them; some models let you truncate (Matryoshka-style) |
| Hosting | Hosted API (simple, data leaves your network) vs open model on your own hardware |
| Cost and latency | Per-token price for the corpus and per query; network round trip for each query |

A small open model running locally can match a hosted one on narrow English corpora; hosted models often do better on mixed languages and domains. Only your eval tells you which.

## One index, one model

Vectors from different models live in unrelated spaces. Comparing a query embedded with model B against documents embedded with model A gives meaningless scores, and nothing raises an error. Rules:

- Store the model name and version with every vector.
- Changing the model, its dimensions, or your chunking means re-embedding everything.
- Migrate like a database: build a new index alongside the old, backfill, compare on your eval set, switch reads with a flag, then delete the old one.

## Try it

Embed these five sentences and print the cosine similarity of the query against each: query "How do I reset my password?"; candidates "Steps to change a forgotten login password", "Password must be 12 characters", "How do I reset my router?", "Resetting your credentials", "Our office is closed on Monday".

Expect "Steps to change a forgotten login password" and "Resetting your credentials" near the top, "Password must be 12 characters" in the middle (shares the topic, not the intent), "How do I reset my router?" surprisingly high (shares the structure "how do I reset"), and the office sentence lowest. The router case is the lesson: similarity measures overall resemblance, not whether the text answers the question, which is why reranking exists.

## Check yourself

1. Why might "refunds are not available" rank highly for the query "are refunds available"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Embeddings capture topic and wording more strongly than negation, so the two sentences sit close together even though they mean opposite things.
</details>

2. What happens if you upgrade the query embedding model but not the document index?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The query vectors live in a different space from the document vectors, so similarity scores become meaningless and retrieval quietly degrades with no error.
</details>

3. Why test models on your own questions rather than trusting a leaderboard?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Leaderboards average over public datasets that may not resemble your domain, languages or question style; the ranking on your data can differ.
</details>
