# Long-context strategies

Context windows of hundreds of thousands to a million or more tokens are now common. That changes what is
possible: a whole codebase, a 600-page contract or a year of support tickets can fit in one call. It does not
remove the need to decide what goes in. This lesson is about choosing a strategy when the material is very long.

## Big windows don't fix it

Four reasons a bigger window is not a free answer:

- **Accuracy drops with length.** Models that find a single planted fact in a huge prompt ("needle in a haystack")
  often do worse on realistic tasks that need several facts combined or reasoning across the text. Benchmarks such
  as RULER were built to show this gap.
- **Cost scales with input.** A million-token prompt sent on every turn of a chat is expensive and slow, even with
  caching.
- **Latency.** Time to first token grows with the prompt. Users notice.
- **Noise.** The more you include, the more chances for outdated or conflicting text to mislead.

## Working around the middle

Models tend to use information at the start and end of a long prompt better than information in the middle. You
can work with that:

- Put **instructions at the top** and **the question at the bottom**, after the material. For long documents,
  restating the key task at the end often helps.
- Place the **most relevant passages near the end**, close to the question, rather than in retrieval order.
- **Structure** long material with headings, ids and tags (`<doc id="12" title="...">`) so the model can navigate.
- Ask the model to **quote the relevant passages first**, then answer from those quotes. This makes it locate
  evidence before reasoning and gives you something to check.

```python
def reorder_for_long_context(chunks: list[dict]) -> list[dict]:
    """chunks sorted by relevance, best first. Put best ones at the edges, weakest in the middle."""
    front, back = [], []
    for i, c in enumerate(chunks):
        (front if i % 2 else back).append(c)
    return front + back[::-1]    # best chunk ends up last, second-best first
```

## Stuff, retrieve or summarise

| Strategy | How it works | Good for | Weak at |
|----------|--------------|----------|---------|
| Full stuffing | Put the whole source in the window | One document that fits, questions needing the whole thing (review a contract) | Cost per call, repeated questions |
| Retrieval over history/docs | Index the material, fetch relevant parts per question | Many questions over a large corpus or long chat history | Questions that need everything ("what changed across all versions?") |
| Summarisation | Replace the material with a summary | Gist questions, long conversations | Exact details |
| Hierarchical | Summaries at several levels, drill down when needed | Very long documents, books, multi-day agent runs | Build cost, complexity |
| Selective loading | Load sections by structure (chapter, file, table) on demand | Code, manuals, anything with a clear map | Needs a table of contents or index |

These combine. A common pattern for a large codebase: always include a short map (file tree plus one line per
module), let the agent load files on demand, and search when it doesn't know where to look. Coding agents work this
way rather than loading the entire repository.

## More or better

A simple decision guide:

1. **Does the source fit comfortably** (say under a quarter of the window) and is it used for one or a few calls?
   Stuff it. It is simpler and avoids retrieval misses.
2. **Will many questions hit the same large source?** Retrieve, or stuff once with prompt caching if the questions
   genuinely need the whole thing.
3. **Does the question need global understanding** (themes, comparisons across the whole source)? Use
   hierarchical summaries or a map-reduce pass over sections.
4. **Does the answer need exact values?** Retrieve the precise passage; don't rely on summaries.

Then measure. Build 20-30 test questions and compare strategies on accuracy, tokens and latency. The winner is often
not the one you expect.

## Try it

You have a 400-page product manual (about 250k tokens) and a chatbot that answers customer questions about it,
roughly 5,000 questions a day. Choose a strategy and justify it on cost and accuracy. Then name one kind of question
where your choice would do badly, and how you would handle it.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Retrieval is the default: stuffing 250k tokens on 5,000 calls a day is over a billion input tokens daily, and even
with caching the latency per call is high. Index the manual by section with headings as metadata, retrieve and
rerank, and add a short table of contents to every prompt so the model knows what exists. Weak case: questions that
need the whole manual, such as "list every feature that requires the Pro plan". Handle those by precomputing
answers offline (a map-reduce pass over all sections into a structured table), or by routing that rare question type
to a long-context call over the relevant chapters.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. A model finds a single planted sentence in a 500k-token prompt. Why doesn't that prove it handles long context
   well for your task?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Finding one literal match is much easier than combining several facts, tracking state or reasoning across the
text. Accuracy on realistic multi-fact tasks drops with length even when simple retrieval tests look perfect.
</details>

2. Where should the most relevant passages go in a long prompt, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Near the end, close to the question (or at the very start), not in the middle. Models tend to use information at the
edges of the prompt more reliably.
</details>

3. When is full stuffing a better choice than retrieval?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

When the source fits comfortably, is used for few calls, and the task needs all of it (for example reviewing a
whole contract), so retrieval would risk missing parts.
</details>
