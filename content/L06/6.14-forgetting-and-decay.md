# Forgetting and decay: half-life and the Ebbinghaus curve

A memory system that never forgets sounds ideal and works badly. After a year the store holds outdated addresses,
abandoned plans and a thousand near-duplicates, and retrieval starts returning old noise next to new signal. People
forget for good reasons. Agents need rules for it too, and, for personal data, sometimes the law requires it.

## Why forget on purpose

- **Stale facts** mislead: the old phone number, last season's preferences.
- **Noise** crowds out signal: more memories means more irrelevant near-matches.
- **Cost**: storage, indexing and retrieval grow with the store.
- **Privacy and trust**: users can ask to be forgotten, and data protection rules (such as India's DPDP Act and the EU's
  GDPR) include rights to erasure and limits on how long you keep data.

## The forgetting curve

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how quickly people forget material they have learned. Retention falls fast at first
and then slowly, approximately:

**R = e^(−t / S)**

where *t* is the time since the memory was last reinforced and *S* is its **strength** (a larger S means slower
forgetting). Engineers often use the equivalent **half-life** form, which is easier to reason about:

**strength = 0.5^(t / h)**

where *h* is the half-life, the time it takes the score to halve. With *h* = 30 days: after 30 days a memory scores 0.5,
after 60 days 0.25, after 90 days 0.125.

```python
def retention(age_days: float, half_life_days: float) -> float:
    return 0.5 ** (age_days / half_life_days)

retention(30, 30)   # 0.5
retention(90, 30)   # 0.125
```

Do not treat these numbers as science for LLMs; the curve comes from human experiments. It is a useful, tunable shape,
not a law. Choose half-lives by memory type: hours for chit-chat, weeks for episodes, months for preferences, none for
a legal name.

## Reinforcement

In people, recalling something makes it stick. Do the same: every time a memory is retrieved *and used*, reset its
clock and lengthen its half-life.

```python
def reinforce(mem, now):
    mem["last_used_at"] = now
    mem["half_life_days"] = min(mem["half_life_days"] * 1.5, 365)   # cap growth
    mem["use_count"] += 1
```

Combined with the recall score from 6.7 (similarity, recency, importance), useful memories stay near the top and unused
ones sink. Importance can set the *starting* half-life: "allergic to peanuts" decays slowly; "wants a quiet table
tonight" decays in a day.

## Pruning policies

Decay is a score. A **policy** turns a score into an action:

| Policy | Rule | Good for |
|--------|------|----------|
| Time-to-live (TTL) | delete after N days | temporary context, session data |
| Least recently used | evict the stalest when full | bounded stores |
| Score threshold | archive when retention × importance < 0.05 | long-lived stores |
| Archive, don't delete | move to cold storage, exclude from retrieval | anything you may need to audit |

Run pruning as a scheduled job (like the pipelines in 4.2). Archive first and delete after a grace period, so a bug in
your scoring does not erase real data.

## Deletion you can audit

Forgetting by decay is soft. A user's request to be forgotten is a **hard delete** that must reach every copy: the
vector store, the entity table, episode logs, summaries, caches, backups on their rotation, and analytics exports.
Design for it from the start by keying everything on `user_id`, and keep a minimal audit record (that a deletion
occurred, when, by whom, not the deleted content). Test it: delete a test user, then search every store for their
data.

## Try it

A memory store holds a user's "prefers aisle seats" (last used 200 days ago, half-life 90 days, importance 0.6) and
"wants a quiet table tonight" (written 3 days ago, half-life 1 day, importance 0.2). Compute each retention and decide
which to prune at a threshold of retention × importance < 0.05.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Aisle seats: 0.5^(200/90) ≈ 0.5^2.22 ≈ 0.21; × 0.6 ≈ 0.13, so keep. Quiet table: 0.5^(3/1) = 0.125; × 0.2 = 0.025, so
archive it. The seat preference survives because importance and a longer half-life offset the age; the one-night request
has expired for good reason.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. What does a half-life of 30 days mean for a memory's score?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Its score halves every 30 days since it was last reinforced: 0.5 after 30 days, 0.25 after 60, 0.125 after 90.
</details>

2. Why reset or extend a memory's half-life when it is used?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Memories that keep proving useful should last longer, while unused ones fade; reinforcement implements that by letting
use counteract decay.
</details>

3. Why is "forget on request" harder than decay?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is a hard delete that must reach every store and copy (vectors, tables, summaries, caches, backups), so it needs
consistent user keys, an audit record and a test that confirms nothing remains.
</details>
