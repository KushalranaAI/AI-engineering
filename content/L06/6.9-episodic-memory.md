# Episodic memory: time-aware session recall

Ask a friend, "What did we decide last time we planned a trip?" and they recall an **episode**: an event with a
time, a context and an outcome. That is different from knowing a fact ("you like trains") or holding an entity record
(your passport number). **Episodic memory** lets an agent say "Last Tuesday we compared two flights and you chose the
6 am one because of the price" and answer questions that depend on *when* something happened.

## What an episode is

A fact is timeless; an episode is anchored. Store the story, not just the conclusion:

```python
episode = {
    "id": "ep_2026_03_09_a",
    "user_id": "u_42",
    "session_id": "s_8831",
    "started_at": "2026-03-09T18:20:00+05:30",
    "ended_at":   "2026-03-09T18:41:00+05:30",
    "summary": "Compared IndiGo 6 am (₹4,900) and Air India 9 am (₹6,200) Delhi-Goa for 14 Mar. User chose IndiGo.",
    "topics": ["flights", "goa", "booking"],
    "outcome": "decision: IndiGo 6 am; booking not yet made",
    "embedding": embed(summary),
}
```

The fields answer the questions an episode must answer: **what** happened (summary), **when** (timestamps), **where**
(session), **what came of it** (outcome).

## Session summaries

Write the episode when a session ends (or after a long idle gap). You already know how from 6.6: run a summariser
over the session's transcript, this time asking for a structured result with a topic list and an outcome line. Keep
the raw transcript in cold storage and link it by `session_id`, so a person (or a later retrieval step) can open the
original if the summary is too thin.

## Time-aware retrieval

Users ask about time in natural language: "what did we say yesterday", "the first time I mentioned the visa", "this
month". Plain vector search ignores time, so handle it explicitly:

1. **Parse the time expression** into a range. Let the model do it, giving it today's date: `"last Tuesday" →
   {"from": "2026-03-03", "to": "2026-03-04"}`.
2. **Filter** episodes by `started_at` in that range (a metadata filter, as in 4.6), then rank by similarity.
3. With no explicit time, **weight recency**: score = similarity × a decay on age (the half-life idea from 6.14).

```python
def recall_episodes(user_id, query, time_range=None, k=3):
    flt = {"user_id": user_id}
    if time_range:
        flt["started_at"] = {"$gte": time_range[0], "$lt": time_range[1]}
    hits = vector_search(embed(query), filter=flt, k=10)
    return rerank_by_recency(hits)[:k]
```

Convert times carefully. Store timestamps with a timezone, and resolve "yesterday" in the *user's* timezone, not the
server's. A late-night chat in Mumbai is already "tomorrow" in UTC.

## Using episodes in a reply

Put two or three relevant episodes into the prompt as a labelled block with their dates, and instruct the model to
refer to them naturally and to say so when it is unsure: "Last Monday you shortlisted two hotels; I can reopen that
list". Do not paste whole transcripts. If no episode matches, the model should say it does not recall a previous
conversation about that rather than invent one.

## Episodes or facts

Episodes are rich but bulky and tied to a moment. Many are worth keeping only for their lesson ("this user always
books the cheapest morning flight"). That distilling step is the subject of the next lesson on semantic memory
(6.10). A useful rule: keep episodes for a limited time (say 6-12 months) and let the distilled facts live longer,
subject to the decay rules in 6.14.

## Try it

A user says, "Continue what we were doing on Friday." It is Monday 9 March 2026 in India. Describe how the agent finds
the episode and what it must do if two episodes happened on Friday.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Resolve "Friday" to 6 March 2026 in Asia/Kolkata and filter episodes to that date. If one matches, summarise it and
resume. If two match, either choose the one whose outcome was unfinished (the outcome field says "not yet booked"),
or ask a short question: "On Friday we did the Goa flights and the visa checklist. Which one?" Never guess between
them silently.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. How does an episode differ from a fact in memory?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

An episode records a specific event with its time, context and outcome. A fact is a general statement without a
particular moment attached.
</details>

2. Why can plain vector similarity not answer "what did we discuss last Tuesday"?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Embeddings capture meaning, not dates. You must turn the time expression into a date range and filter on stored
timestamps, then rank within that range.
</details>

3. Why store timestamps with a timezone?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Relative words like "yesterday" depend on the user's local day. Without a timezone, evening chats can land on the
wrong date and the wrong episode is recalled.
</details>
