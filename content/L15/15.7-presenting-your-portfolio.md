# Presenting your portfolio

A reviewer screening AI engineering candidates might spend two minutes on your GitHub. In that time they need to see what you built, that it really runs, and that you made engineering decisions rather than following a tutorial. The work in your projects is done; this lesson is about making it legible.

## Choose what to show

Three to five strong projects beat ten half-finished ones. Pick by the roles you want:

| Target role | Lead with |
|-------------|-----------|
| AI engineer at a product company | Document Q&A platform, monitored API, MCP server |
| Agent-focused team | Multi-agent research system, MCP server, screening agent |
| Applied ML / model-adjacent | Fine-tune vs prompt baseline, voice or vision build |

Pin those repositories on your GitHub profile. Archive or make private the tutorial clones and abandoned experiments; they dilute the signal.

## A README a reviewer can scan

Put the evidence above the setup instructions. A structure that works:

```markdown
# DocQA: question answering over your own documents

**Live demo:** https://... · **2-min video:** https://...

Upload PDFs or web pages and ask questions; answers cite their sources
and decline when the evidence is thin.

![architecture](docs/architecture.png)

## Results
| Retriever | Hit rate @5 | MRR | p95 latency | $ / 1k questions |
|-----------|-------------|-----|-------------|------------------|
| Dense     | 0.78        | 0.61| 420 ms      | 0.90             |
| Hybrid + rerank | 0.91  | 0.77| 610 ms      | 1.40             |

## Key decisions
- Hybrid retrieval: product codes failed with dense-only (see eval/misses.md).
- Reranker adds 190 ms but lifts hit rate by 13 points; kept it.

## What didn't work
- Semantic chunking: no gain over recursive splitting, 4x slower ingestion.

## Run it locally
...
```

The results table and "key decisions" are what separate you from people who followed a tutorial. Numbers should come from a script in the repo, so anyone can reproduce them.

## Demos that load fast

- **A short video** (90 seconds to 3 minutes): the problem in one sentence, the demo on realistic input, one hard case, and the results table. Reviewers will watch this more than they will run your code.
- **A live demo** that stays up: put a spending cap on API keys, rate-limit by IP, use a small model for the public version if needed, and add a health check so you notice when it breaks. A dead link reads worse than no link; if you can't keep it alive, say "demo on request" and rely on the video.
- **Seed data**: preload a sample corpus or example inputs so a visitor sees value in one click.

## Write up decisions and failures

For each project, keep a short write-up (in the README or a blog post) covering:

1. The problem and why it is hard.
2. The baseline and how you measured it.
3. Two or three decisions with the trade-off you accepted.
4. What failed and what you changed.
5. What you would do with more time or at 100x the traffic.

This doubles as interview preparation: it is the story you will tell when someone asks you to walk through a project.

## Put it in front of people

- **CV**: one line per project with a number. "Built a document Q&A service with hybrid retrieval and reranking; raised hit rate from 0.78 to 0.91 at $1.40 per 1,000 questions."
- **GitHub profile README**: a short intro and links to the pinned projects.
- **One post per project** on a blog or LinkedIn, focused on a single lesson learned ("Why my RAG failed on product codes"). Specific lessons get read and shared; "I built a chatbot" does not.

A small script can help you keep the CV line and README table in sync with your latest eval run:

```python
import json
r = json.load(open("eval/results/latest.json"))
best = max(r["runs"], key=lambda x: x["hit_rate_at_5"])
print(f"Raised hit rate from {r['baseline']['hit_rate_at_5']:.2f} to "
      f"{best['hit_rate_at_5']:.2f} at ${best['cost_per_1k']:.2f} per 1,000 questions.")
```

## Try it

Pick your strongest project. Ask someone who has not seen it to open the repository and, with a two-minute timer, tell you: what it does, whether it works, and one decision you made. Fix whatever they couldn't find.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Typical gaps: the live link or video is buried below installation steps; there's no results table, so "whether it works" is unanswerable; decisions are hidden in code comments. Move the demo link, a one-line description, the diagram and the results table to the top, and add a "Key decisions" section of three bullets. Rerun the test with someone else.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why put the results table above the setup instructions?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Reviewers skim from the top and decide in a minute or two. Evidence that the system works and was measured is what they are looking for; setup steps matter only to the few who run it.
</details>

2. Your live demo costs too much to keep running. What are your options?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Cap spending and rate-limit; use a cheaper model for the public version; cache common questions; or take it down and rely on a recorded video with "live demo on request".
</details>

3. Rewrite "Built a RAG chatbot using LangChain" as a stronger CV line.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Something like: "Built and deployed a document Q&A service with hybrid retrieval and reranking; raised retrieval hit rate from 0.78 to 0.91 on a 40-question eval at about $1.40 per 1,000 questions." It names the problem, a decision and a measured result.
</details>
