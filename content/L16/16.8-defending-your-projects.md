# Defending your projects

In most AI engineering loops at least one round is about your own work. The interviewer will pick a project and keep
asking "why?" until they find the edge of your understanding. Showing that it works is the easy part. The round is
won by explaining your decisions, your numbers and your mistakes.

## Choose two or three

Pick projects that are deployed, that you built most of yourself, and that match the role. One RAG-heavy, one
agent or tool-heavy, one with an operations story covers most postings. Depth beats breadth: two projects you can
defend for 30 minutes each are worth more than six you can describe for three.

## A structure for the story

Tell each project in this order. It works for a two-minute version and stretches to twenty.

| Part | One-line example (document Q&A platform) |
|------|-------------------------------------------|
| Problem | Engineers wasted time searching 4,000 internal design docs |
| Requirements | Answers in under 5 s, cite sources, respect team permissions |
| Baseline | Dense retrieval + one model call: 62% hit@5 on 50 test questions |
| Architecture | Ingestion queue, hybrid retrieval, reranker, FastAPI, Postgres + pgvector |
| Decisions | Hybrid because 30% of questions contained ticket ids |
| Trade-offs | Reranker added 400 ms but raised hit@5 to 84% |
| Failures | Tables in PDFs were garbled; fixed with a layout-aware parser |
| Evals | 50-question retrieval set, LLM judge on 30 answers, checked against my own labels |
| Results | 84% hit@5, p95 3.1 s, about $0.004 per question |

Notice every line has either a reason or a number. Write this table for each project and keep it next to you while
practising.

## Justify every choice

For each component, be ready with: what you chose, what you compared it with, and why it won. "I used LangChain
because it's popular" fails. "I started hand-written, moved retrieval to a framework for its loaders, kept the
generation step hand-written because I needed control over citations" passes.

## Have the numbers ready

Quality score and how it was measured, latency (p50 and p95), cost per query, data size, traffic if any, and gain
over the baseline. Keep a small script or notebook that regenerates them so you trust them:

```python
summary = {
    "questions": 50,
    "hit@5": {"dense": 0.62, "hybrid": 0.76, "hybrid+rerank": 0.84},
    "p95_latency_s": 3.1,
    "cost_per_query_usd": 0.004,
}
for k, v in summary.items():
    print(f"{k:>20}: {v}")
```

If you don't have a number, say how you would get it. Never invent one: a follow-up question will expose it.

## Own the mistakes

Prepare one real failure per project: what went wrong, how you found it, what you changed. This is often the most
convincing part of the conversation, because only people who really built something have these stories.

## The usual follow-ups

| Question | What a good answer does |
|----------|------------------------|
| "Why didn't you use RAG?" / "Why RAG?" | Points to the nature of the data (changing, private, needs citations) |
| "Why not fine-tune?" | Names the cost and says prompt and retrieval met the bar, with the metric |
| "Why do you need an agent?" | Shows steps depend on intermediate results, or admits a workflow would do |
| "What happens at 100x traffic?" | Names the first bottleneck (rate limits, GPU, DB) and the fix |
| "How would you halve the cost?" | Routing to a smaller model, caching, shorter prompts, fewer chunks, batch jobs |
| "What breaks first?" | Picks a specific component with a reason, not "it scales fine" |

## Common weak answers

- Describing features instead of decisions.
- "We" everywhere, so it's unclear what you did.
- No baseline, so improvements have nothing to be compared with.
- Defensiveness when challenged. "That's a fair point, I'd test it by..." is stronger.

## Practice drill

Record a two-minute version and a ten-minute version of each project. Then ask a friend, or a model with
instructions to be a tough interviewer, to ask the six follow-ups above, one after another, and record your answers.

## Check yourself

1. Why does a baseline matter so much in a project defence?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Without it, no improvement can be quantified and no design choice can be shown to have helped. It turns "I added a
reranker" into "a reranker added 8 points of hit rate for 400 ms".
</details>

2. How should you answer "why not fine-tune?" if you didn't?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Say what metric the system needed to hit, that prompting and retrieval met it, and that tuning would add data,
training, serving and retraining costs with no measured failure to fix.
</details>

3. What should you do if you don't know a number the interviewer asks for?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Say so and explain exactly how you would measure it. Guessing or inventing a figure is worse than admitting the gap.
</details>
