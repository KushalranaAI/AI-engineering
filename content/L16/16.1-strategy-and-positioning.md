# Strategy and positioning

You cannot prepare for every tool on every job post, and you don't need to. AI engineering interviews have settled
into a recognisable shape, and most postings test a handful of areas in depth. This lesson is about spending your
preparation time where the marks are, and pitching the skills you already have at the role in front of you.

## What interviews test now

A few years ago a GenAI interview could be a quiz: what is attention, what is temperature, define RAG. Those
questions still appear as warm-ups, but the weight has moved to **scenarios**: design this system, debug this
failure, defend this choice. A typical loop looks like this:

| Round | What they are checking | Where you learned it |
|-------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Recruiter / hiring manager | Fit, motivation, can you explain your projects simply | Your portfolio |
| Coding | Ordinary software engineering, often with an LLM API in the mix | Python, APIs |
| AI system design | Requirements to architecture, trade-offs, failure modes | RAG, agents, LLMOps |
| Scenario / debugging | Calm, structured diagnosis of a broken system | Evals, observability |
| Project deep-dive | Did you really build it, and do you understand why it works | Your portfolio |
| Behavioural | Ownership, collaboration, handling ambiguity | Your experience |

Notice how much of that is the **engineering layer**: APIs, inference, deployment, monitoring, cost, reliability.
Companies hiring AI engineers mostly want someone who can ship and operate an AI feature, not someone who can derive
backpropagation.

## Decoding a job description

Read a posting as a list of signals, not a shopping list. Group each line into a roadmap area and count.

```python
jd_lines = [
    "Build RAG pipelines over internal documentation",
    "Experience with LangGraph or similar agent frameworks",
    "Own evaluation and monitoring of LLM features",
    "Familiarity with AWS (Bedrock a plus)",
    "Python, FastAPI, Postgres",
]
areas = {
    "rag": ["rag", "retrieval", "search", "embedding"],
    "agents": ["agent", "langgraph", "tool"],
    "evals_ops": ["evaluation", "monitoring", "observability"],
    "platform": ["aws", "azure", "gcp", "bedrock", "vertex"],
}
score = {a: sum(any(k in l.lower() for k in kws) for l in jd_lines) for a, kws in areas.items()}
print(score)  # {'rag': 1, 'agents': 1, 'evals_ops': 1, 'platform': 1}
```

The code is a toy; the habit is the point. For a real posting, mark the two or three areas that appear repeatedly or
in the first lines. Those are your deep-preparation topics. The rest ("a plus", tool names in a long list) get a
skim.

## Role flavours

| Role | Emphasis | Typical deep question |
|------|----------|----------------------|
| AI engineer | Building and running LLM features in products | "Design a support assistant for 50k users" |
| GenAI data scientist | Evals, experiments, data, sometimes fine-tuning | "How would you know the new prompt is better?" |
| Data scientist (classic) | Statistics, ML models, analysis | "How would you set up this A/B test?" |

These overlap heavily. Position yourself by emphasis: an engineer moving in should lead with systems and shipping; a
data scientist moving in should lead with evaluation rigour and then show they can deploy.

## Hosted APIs vs open weights

Almost every loop asks some version of "would you call a provider API or run your own model?" A strong answer
covers six axes and lands on a decision for the stated context:

| Axis | Hosted API | Self-hosted open-weight |
|------|-----------|-------------------------|
| Capability | Usually the strongest models | Close behind for many tasks, weaker at the frontier |
| Cost | Per token, zero idle cost | GPU cost whether busy or not; cheaper at steady high volume |
| Latency | Network hop, shared capacity | You control it, but you must tune it |
| Control | Model can change or be retired | You pin the exact weights |
| Privacy | Data leaves your network (contracts help) | Data stays in |
| Operations | Almost none | GPUs, scaling, upgrades, on-call |

**Weak answer:** "Open source is cheaper and more private." It ignores idle GPU cost, operations and quality.
**Strong answer:** "Start on an API to learn the task and build evals. Move a workload in-house when volume is high
and steady, data rules require it, or a small tuned model matches quality, and only after the evals prove it."

## A personal plan

Rate yourself 1-5 on each area the posting tests, multiply by how much it is weighted, and fix the biggest gaps
first. Two weeks is a common window: spend most of it on mock scenarios and system design, not rereading notes.

## Try it

Take one real AI engineer job posting. Classify every requirement line into an area, pick the top three areas, and
write a 10-day plan with one mock exercise per day.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

A good plan names concrete outputs, not topics. For a posting heavy on RAG, evals and AWS, for example: day 1-3,
whiteboard two RAG designs and one "quality dropped" debug, out loud and timed; day 4-5, rehearse explaining your
eval setup and one metric you tracked; day 6, sketch the same design on the cloud provider's managed services; day
7-8, two full project defences; day 9, a mock loop with a partner; day 10, review recordings and patch the weakest
answers. Tool-name memorising should be close to zero.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why do scenario questions dominate over definition questions in current AI engineering interviews?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because the job is mostly building and operating AI features. Definitions are easy to memorise and say little about
whether you can design, debug and ship; scenarios expose how you reason about trade-offs and failures.
</details>

2. A posting lists twelve tools. How do you decide what to study?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Group the lines into areas, weight the ones repeated or listed first, and prepare those areas in depth. Tools
named once in a long list are usually interchangeable; knowing the concept behind them is enough.
</details>

3. Give a one-sentence default for hosted vs self-hosted models.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Start hosted to learn the task and build evals, and self-host when steady volume, data restrictions or a proven
small-model match make it pay for its operational burden.
</details>
