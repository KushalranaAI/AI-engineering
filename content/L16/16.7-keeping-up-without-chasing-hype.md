# Keeping up without chasing hype

New models, frameworks and papers arrive every week. Interviewers ask about this partly out of curiosity and partly
as a judgment test: do you switch tools because a post went viral, or do you have a process? The best answers sound
like an engineer evaluating a dependency, not a fan following a feed.

## Example questions

- "How do you stay current in GenAI?"
- "A new model just topped a leaderboard. Would you switch our production system to it?"
- "What's something recent you think is overhyped? Underhyped?"

## A process for anything new

Ask the same four questions of every model, framework, paper or tool:

| Question | Example: a new open-weight model | Example: a new agent framework |
|----------|-------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| What changed, concretely? | Better at tool use, longer context, cheaper per token | New abstraction for state and retries |
| Does it touch a problem I have? | Our tool-calling error rate is our top failure | We hand-roll retries and it's fine |
| What evidence exists? | Benchmarks, independent reproductions, my own evals | Docs, production users, issue tracker health |
| What does adopting it cost? | Re-run evals, prompt tweaks, serving changes | Rewrite, lock-in, new failure modes |

**Real change** tends to show up as a step in capability or cost that holds on independent tests, or an
infrastructure shift many teams adopt (a protocol, a serving technique). **Hype** tends to be one benchmark, one demo,
or a new name for an old idea.

## Evaluating a new model for production

Interviewers love this one because it ties together evals, cost and rollout. A strong answer is a pipeline:

1. **Public benchmarks** to shortlist, knowing they can be contaminated or unrepresentative.
2. **Your own eval set**: real production questions with graded answers, the same judge and metrics as the current
   model.
3. **Cost and latency** at your actual prompt and output lengths, not list prices alone.
4. **Prompt adaptation**: a new model may need prompt changes; budget a little time for it, and compare
   best-prompt vs best-prompt.
5. **Controlled rollout**: shadow traffic, then a small canary, watching quality signals and errors, with instant
   rollback.

```python
import statistics as st

def compare(results_old: list[dict], results_new: list[dict]) -> None:
    for name, rs in [("current", results_old), ("candidate", results_new)]:
        print(name,
              f"score={st.mean(r['score'] for r in rs):.2f}",
              f"p95_ms={sorted(r['latency_ms'] for r in rs)[int(0.95 * len(rs))]}",
              f"cost_per_1k=${1000 * st.mean(r['cost_usd'] for r in rs):.2f}")
    worse = [a["id"] for a, b in zip(results_old, results_new) if b["score"] < a["score"]]
    print(f"{len(worse)} questions got worse; read them before deciding")
```

The last line matters: an average improvement can hide a new failure on cases you care about.

## A credible "how do you stay current?"

**Weak answer:** "I follow lots of people on social media and try every new tool." It signals noise, not judgment.

**Strong answer** has three parts: sources, a filter, and a habit.

- **Sources**: a few primary ones: provider release notes and docs, papers for the areas you work in, a couple of
  practitioners who publish measurements.
- **Filter**: "I only go deep when something touches a problem I have, and I check it against my own evals."
- **Habit, with an example**: "When a new small model came out, I ran it through my document-extraction eval over a
  weekend. It matched the larger model on 90% of cases at a fraction of the cost, so I wrote up a routing proposal."

The example is what makes the answer believable. Keep one or two ready from your own projects.

## Overhyped and underhyped

Have a reasoned opinion on each, and keep it moderate. For example: autonomous multi-agent systems for simple tasks
are often overhyped (a workflow does it cheaper); evals and good retrieval are underhyped (they decide most real
outcomes). Back each with one sentence of reasoning.

## Check yourself

1. Why aren't public benchmarks enough to switch a production model?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

They may not represent your task or data, can be contaminated by training data, and say nothing about your cost,
latency or prompt fit. Your own eval set is what predicts your outcomes.
</details>

2. What makes a "how do you stay current" answer credible?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A small set of primary sources, an explicit filter tied to real problems, and a concrete example of evaluating
something new and acting on the result.
</details>

3. What should the last step before full rollout of a new model be?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A controlled rollout: shadow or canary traffic with quality and error monitoring and an immediate rollback path.
</details>
