# Communicating in interviews

An interviewer can only grade the thinking they can see. Many capable engineers lose offers because their reasoning
stays in their heads, comes out in the wrong order, or disappears under implementation detail. This lesson is about
making your thinking visible.

## Pick the right altitude

Match the depth of the answer to the question. A useful habit: answer in one sentence, then offer to go deeper.

| Question | Too low | Right altitude |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| "How does your retrieval work?" | Walking through function names and config flags | "Hybrid: BM25 plus vectors, fused with RRF, then a reranker. Want me to go into why hybrid?" |
| "Why did latency improve?" | "I changed some settings" | "We moved the reranker to a GPU and cut candidates from 100 to 40; p95 went from 5 s to 2.8 s." |

## State assumptions before solving

When a question is ambiguous, say what you are assuming, then solve. "I'll assume around 1,000 users and English
only; tell me if that's off." It takes ten seconds, shows you noticed the ambiguity, and invites correction before you
build on a wrong premise.

## Trade-offs before tools

Name the options and what each costs, then choose. Jumping to a tool sounds like pattern matching.

**Weak:** "I'd use a vector database."
**Strong:** "Two options: Postgres with pgvector, which keeps everything in one database we already run, or a
dedicated vector store, which scales further and filters faster at very large sizes. At 2 million chunks I'd start with
pgvector and revisit if query latency or filtering becomes a problem."

A simple sentence frame helps: "Options are A and B. A gives X but costs Y. Given our constraint Z, I'd pick A, and I'd
switch if W."

## Build the diagram up

On a whiteboard, start with three boxes (user, your service, model) and one arrow. Add each component only as you
explain why it is needed. Label arrows with what flows (query, chunks, tokens) and, where useful, a number (latency,
rate). A diagram revealed piece by piece is easy to follow; a full diagram drawn in silence is not.

## Measure, don't bluff

You will be asked things you don't know. The strong move is to turn the gap into a method:

> "I haven't measured that. I'd expect the reranker to dominate latency, but I'd confirm by tracing a hundred
> requests and looking at per-span timings."

Bluffing is risky because interviewers often ask follow-ups precisely where they sense uncertainty.

## Experience stories

For "tell me about a time..." questions, use five beats: **situation, decision, implementation, result, lesson**.
The decision beat is what most people skip, and it is what an engineering interviewer most wants.

| Beat | Example |
|------|---------|
| Situation | Our support bot gave confident wrong answers on pricing questions |
| Decision | Add abstention on weak retrieval rather than a bigger model, because the bad answers had poor evidence |
| Implementation | Threshold on reranker score, tuned on 80 labelled questions |
| Result | Wrong-answer rate on pricing fell from 18% to 4%; 9% of questions now get "I don't know" plus a handoff |
| Lesson | Measure the abstention rate too, or you can "fix" accuracy by refusing everything |

Keep each story under two minutes, then stop and let them ask.

## A small self-check script

Record a practice answer, transcribe it (any speech-to-text tool works), and check a few habits automatically:

```python
import re

def answer_habits(transcript: str) -> dict:
    t = transcript.lower()
    return {
        "states_assumption": bool(re.search(r"\b(assum|let's say|i'll take)\w*", t)),
        "names_tradeoff": bool(re.search(r"\b(trade-?off|on the other hand|costs?|but)\b", t)),
        "uses_numbers": len(re.findall(r"\d", t)) >= 3,
        "filler_per_100_words": round(100 * len(re.findall(r"\b(um|uh|like|basically)\b", t)) / max(1, len(t.split())), 1),
    }
```

It is crude, but trends across recordings are useful.

## Common weak answers

- Starting to design before asking anything.
- Long silences while thinking. Narrate instead: "I'm weighing whether caching helps here..."
- Answering a different, easier question than the one asked.

## Practice drill

Take five questions from earlier lessons. For each, give a 30-second answer, then a 3-minute answer, recorded. Check
that the short one stands alone and the long one opens with assumptions and names at least one trade-off.

## Check yourself

1. Why state assumptions out loud?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It shows you spotted the ambiguity, lets the interviewer correct you early, and makes the rest of your reasoning easy
to follow.
</details>

2. What beat do people most often skip in experience stories?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The decision: what options existed and why they chose one. It is the part that shows engineering judgment.
</details>

3. What do you say when you don't know?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Admit it, give your best hypothesis, and describe how you would measure or test it.
</details>
