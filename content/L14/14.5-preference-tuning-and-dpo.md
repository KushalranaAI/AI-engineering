# Preference tuning and DPO

Some qualities are easy to recognise and hard to demonstrate. You can't write "the one correct" concise, warm, well-judged reply to a frustrated customer, but shown two replies you can say which is better. Preference tuning learns from exactly that kind of judgement, and DPO is the simplest way to do it.

## Demonstrations vs preferences

| Data type | One example says | Trains with | Good for |
|-----------|------------------|-------------|----------|
| Demonstration | "For this prompt, this is the answer" | SFT | Formats, tasks with a right answer |
| Preference | "For this prompt, answer A beats answer B" | DPO and relatives | Tone, helpfulness, refusals, concision |

## Building preference pairs

Each record is a prompt, a **chosen** response and a **rejected** response:

```json
{"prompt": [{"role": "user", "content": "My order is 9 days late. This is ridiculous."}],
 "chosen":   [{"role": "assistant", "content": "I'm sorry, that's far too long. I've checked: it's held at the depot. I've asked them to dispatch today and you'll get tracking by 6pm."}],
 "rejected": [{"role": "assistant", "content": "We apologise for any inconvenience caused. Delivery times may vary due to a range of factors. Please allow additional time."}]}
```

Good pairs differ **in the quality you care about** and as little as possible otherwise. Common ways to get candidates: sample two or more responses from your SFT model at a higher temperature, then have a human or a judge pick.

## Where preferences come from

- **Human raters** with a written rubric. Measure inter-rater agreement on a shared sample (for example, percent agreement or Cohen's kappa). If raters agree only 60% of the time, the model is learning noise.
- **Your eval judge** (an LLM judge calibrated against human labels in your eval work). Cheap at volume, inherits the judge's biases.
- **Production feedback**: thumbs up/down, edits users made, which suggestion was accepted. Real but sparse and skewed toward people who bother to click.

## Avoiding accidental lessons

If chosen answers are usually longer, the model learns "longer is better", not "more helpful". Other hidden signals: chosen answers always use bullet points, always apologise, always mention a name. Check simple statistics before training:

```python
import statistics as st
lc = [len(r["chosen"][0]["content"]) for r in pairs]
lr = [len(r["rejected"][0]["content"]) for r in pairs]
print(st.mean(lc), st.mean(lr))   # a large gap means length may be what the model learns
```

Rebalance or rewrite pairs until the only consistent difference is the one you want.

## How DPO works, intuitively

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) skips the separate reward model and reinforcement learning loop that classic RLHF uses. For each pair it compares how likely the model being trained finds the chosen and rejected answers, *relative to a frozen reference copy* (usually the SFT model). The loss pushes the gap in favour of the chosen answer to grow. The reference keeps the model from drifting too far: it can't just inflate one answer's probability by going off the rails. A parameter usually called `beta` controls how tightly it stays near the reference.

```python
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer

trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model=sft_model,                 # start from your SFT checkpoint
    ref_model=None,                  # TRL makes a frozen copy if not given
    args=DPOConfig(output_dir="out/dpo-v1", beta=0.1, learning_rate=5e-6, num_train_epochs=1),
    train_dataset=pairs_ds,
    processing_class=tokenizer,
)
trainer.train()
```

(Argument names change across TRL versions; check the docs.)

## Why DPO comes after SFT

Preference data says which of two answers is better, not how to produce a good answer in the right format. If the base model can't yet do the task, both candidates are poor and the comparisons teach little. So the usual order is SFT to get the task and format right, then DPO to polish qualities like tone and judgement. DPO learning rates are typically much lower than SFT.

## When SFT is enough

If your eval shows the SFT model already hits the target, or the remaining failures are about correctness (wrong field, wrong label), DPO won't help much; better demonstrations will. Add preference tuning when the remaining gap is about *which of several acceptable answers is preferred*, and you can measure it, for example with a blind pairwise judge.

## Try it

Build 50 preference pairs for one tone goal (for example, "direct and specific, not corporate"). Before training, compute the average length and bullet-point count for chosen vs rejected. Fix any skew, train with DPO starting from your SFT checkpoint, then have a judge that doesn't know which is which pick between 30 before/after pairs.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Most first drafts of a pair set show chosen answers noticeably longer than rejected ones. Rewrite some rejected answers to be long-but-vague and some chosen ones to be short-and-specific. After DPO, a fair blind judge (randomised order, rubric focused on directness) should prefer the tuned model on most prompts; if it prefers it mainly on length, the pairs still leak length.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why does DPO need a reference model?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The loss is defined relative to the reference's probabilities. Without that anchor the model could raise the chosen/rejected gap in degenerate ways and drift far from sensible language; `beta` sets how strongly it is held close.
</details>

2. Raters agree with each other only 55% of the time on your pairs. What should you do before training?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Fix the labelling: tighten the rubric, discuss disagreements, drop ambiguous pairs. Near-chance agreement means the preference signal is mostly noise and the tune will learn little or the wrong thing.
</details>

3. Name one task where SFT is the right tool and one where DPO adds value.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

SFT: extracting fields into a fixed JSON schema (one right answer). DPO: making replies more concise and empathetic, or refusing borderline requests in the preferred way (judged by comparison).
</details>
