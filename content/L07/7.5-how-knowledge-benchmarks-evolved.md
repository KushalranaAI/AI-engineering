# How knowledge benchmarks evolved

Knowledge benchmarks have a short life. A new test is published, models score poorly, a couple of years later the best ones score near the ceiling, and the field needs something harder. Following that history tells you which tests still mean something today and why.

## The early broad tests

**MMLU** (Massive Multitask Language Understanding, 2020) gathered about 16,000 four-option multiple-choice questions across 57 subjects, from elementary maths to law and medicine. It became the headline knowledge number for years because it was broad and easy to run: pick A-D, compare with the key.

**TruthfulQA** (2021) asked a different question: does the model repeat popular misconceptions? Its 817 questions are written so that the most common human answer is false ("What happens if you crack your knuckles a lot?"). It showed that larger models of that time were sometimes *less* truthful, because they imitated common text more faithfully.

## Human exams as benchmarks

**AGIEval** (2023) took questions from real standardised exams taken by people: college entrance tests, law school admission, maths competitions, civil service exams. The appeal is a direct human comparison: "the model scored above the average test-taker". The caveat is that exam questions are widely published online, which raises contamination risk.

## The harder generation

As frontier models reached high scores on MMLU, newer sets raised the bar:

| Benchmark | What changed | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **MMLU-Pro** (2024) | Ten answer options instead of four; more reasoning-heavy questions; noisy items removed | Guessing is less rewarded; scores spread out again |
| **GPQA** (2023) | Graduate-level biology, physics and chemistry questions written by domain experts and designed to be "Google-proof" | Skilled non-experts with web access score low, so it tests real expertise |
| **SimpleQA** (2024) | Short factual questions with one indisputable answer; scores correct, wrong and "not attempted" separately | Measures factual reliability and whether the model knows when to abstain |
| **Humanity's Last Exam** (2025) | Thousands of very hard questions from experts across many fields, kept only if top models failed them at creation time | Leaves large headroom at the frontier |

Notice the two directions. One is **harder** (GPQA, HLE): questions beyond a typical educated person. The other is **more reliable** (SimpleQA, TruthfulQA): not "how much do you know" but "how often are you confidently wrong". For most applications the second matters more.

## Why the bar keeps rising

Three forces retire benchmarks:

1. **Saturation.** When the best models score in the high 80s or 90s, the remaining errors are often mislabelled questions. Differences between models fall inside noise.
2. **Contamination.** Public questions end up in web crawls and training data.
3. **Optimisation pressure.** Once a number is headline marketing, labs tune toward it.

A useful habit: when you see a score, check the benchmark's current ceiling. A 2-point lead on a saturated test means little; the same lead on a test where top models score 30% may be meaningful.

Worked example of reading SimpleQA-style results:

| Model | Correct | Wrong | Not attempted |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 45% | 50% | 5% |
| B | 40% | 15% | 45% |

Model A "knows more", but Model B is far safer for a factual product: when it answers, it is right about 73% of the time (40/55), versus about 47% (45/95) for A. Which you want depends on whether a wrong answer is worse than no answer.

```python
def precision_when_attempted(correct, wrong):
    return correct / (correct + wrong)

print(round(precision_when_attempted(45, 50), 2))  # 0.47
print(round(precision_when_attempted(40, 15), 2))  # 0.73
```

You are choosing a model for a medical-information assistant that must avoid confident errors. Which two of the benchmarks above would you look at first, and what would you look for?

## Check yourself

1. What did TruthfulQA measure that MMLU did not?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Whether a model repeats common false beliefs. Its questions are built so that the popular answer is wrong, testing truthfulness rather than breadth of knowledge.
</details>

2. Why does a benchmark become less useful once top models score near its ceiling?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Remaining errors are often label noise, and the gaps between models become smaller than run-to-run variance, so the test no longer separates models. Contamination also becomes more likely with age.
</details>

3. Why does SimpleQA report "not attempted" separately?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

So it can distinguish a model that abstains when unsure from one that guesses. A model with fewer correct answers but far fewer wrong ones can be the better choice where confident errors are costly.
</details>
