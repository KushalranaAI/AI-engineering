# LLM-as-a-judge

Some qualities cannot be checked with code. Is an answer helpful? Polite? Faithful to a source? Does it follow the
brand tone? Human review is the gold standard but does not scale to thousands of cases on every change. **LLM-as-a-judge**
uses a model to grade another model's output against a rubric. It is cheap, fast and surprisingly consistent with human
raters when done carefully, and misleading when done carelessly. This lesson is about doing it carefully.

## When a model can grade a model

Pick the cheapest reliable checker for each criterion:

1. **Code first**: exact match, regex, schema validity, "contains the order id", unit tests. Deterministic and free.
2. **A judge** for open-ended qualities where code cannot decide.
3. **Humans** for calibration, for high-stakes decisions and for what the judge cannot see.

Use a judge to check *properties* ("is the answer supported by the context?"), not to produce ground truth.

## Rubrics and scoring formats

A vague criterion ("is it good?") gives noisy scores. Write a specific rubric and choose a format that fits:

| Format | Example | Notes |
|--------|---------|-------|
| Pass/fail | "Does the answer contain only claims supported by the context?" | Most reliable; start here |
| Small scale (1-5) | tone, helpfulness | Define each level in words with an anchor example |
| Pairwise | "Which answer is better, A or B?" | Good for comparing two versions; use with order swapping |
| Reference-based | "Does the answer match the reference on the key facts?" | Needs a golden answer (7.7) |

Prefer binary or 3-point scales. People and models are bad at telling a 3 from a 4.

## Judge prompts that behave

```python
JUDGE = """You are grading a customer-support answer.

Question: {question}
Context the assistant was given: {context}
Assistant answer: {answer}

Criterion: FAITHFUL. Every factual claim in the answer must be supported by the context.
- PASS: all claims supported, or the answer says the context does not contain the information.
- FAIL: at least one claim is not in the context or contradicts it.

First list each claim and whether the context supports it. Then give your verdict.
Return JSON: {{"claims": [{{"claim": "...", "supported": true}}], "verdict": "PASS|FAIL"}}"""
```

Good habits visible here: reasoning *before* the verdict, a clearly defined criterion, structured output (2.4), and
`temperature=0` when you call it. Add two or three anchored examples (one clear pass, one clear fail, one borderline)
if scores wander.

## Known biases

Judges have predictable weaknesses:

- **Position bias**: in pairwise comparison, the first (or second) answer wins more often. Run each pair twice with the
  order swapped and count only consistent wins.
- **Verbosity bias**: longer answers score higher regardless of quality. State in the rubric that length is not a
  virtue, or compare answers of similar length.
- **Self-preference**: a model tends to favour text written by itself or its family. Judge with a different family from
  the one being judged.
- **Formatting bias**: bullets, bold and confident tone can sway scores.

The paper *Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena* (Zheng et al., 2023) documents these effects and shows
that strong judges agree with human preferences about as often as humans agree with each other, when set up well.

## Calibrating against humans

Never trust a judge you have not checked. Take 50-100 cases, have a person label them, run the judge, and compare:

```python
agree = sum(h == j for h, j in zip(human, judge)) / len(human)     # raw agreement
# for binary labels, also look at the confusion matrix: false passes are usually the costly ones
```

If agreement is low, read the disagreements. Usually the rubric is ambiguous, or the human and the judge are using
different standards. Edit the rubric, add examples, re-run. Report agreement (and kappa from 7.7) alongside the scores
you publish, and re-check whenever you change the judge model or prompt.

## Cost and reliability

Judge calls add up (a 500-case run with three criteria is 1,500 calls). Tame the cost: sample rather than judge everything
in online monitoring, cache results keyed by input and answer, batch requests, and use a cheaper judge first with a
stronger one for the ambiguous cases. Remember that judge scores are **noisy estimates**: on a 100-case set, a pass rate
of 82% has a margin of roughly ±8 points at 95% confidence. Small differences between two prompts are not evidence.

## Try it

You compare two answer styles with a pairwise judge. Version B wins 65 of 100 comparisons. B's answers average 40% longer.
What do you do before believing it?

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Re-run with positions swapped to remove order bias, add a length-neutral instruction to the rubric or compare a
length-matched subset, and check agreement with human labels on a sample of 30. If B still wins after swapping and on
equal-length pairs, the preference is more believable. Also compute a confidence interval: 65/100 is about 65 ± 9 points,
so the win is real but modest.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why ask the judge to reason before giving its verdict?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Listing the checks first makes the verdict follow from evidence rather than a snap impression, and it gives you
reasoning to read when you audit disagreements.
</details>

2. Name two judge biases and one way to counter each.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Position bias: run comparisons both ways and keep consistent wins. Verbosity bias: tell the judge length is not a
virtue or compare length-matched answers. Self-preference: use a judge from a different model family.
</details>

3. How do you know a judge is trustworthy for your task?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Compare its verdicts with human labels on a sample (50-100 cases), measure agreement, fix the rubric where they differ,
and re-check after any change to the judge.
</details>
