# Responsible AI and governance

Security stops attackers. Governance is about the harm your system can do when nobody is attacking it: confident wrong answers, unfair treatment of some groups, decisions nobody can explain or reverse. It is also about proving, to a regulator, auditor or customer, that you thought about those risks. As an engineer you won't write the legal analysis, but you build the things it depends on: documentation, controls, logs and evals.

This lesson is not legal advice. Laws in this area are new and their timelines and guidance keep moving, so treat specifics here as orientation and check current official sources.

## The core concerns

| Concern | What it looks like in an LLM app | Engineering response |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucination | Invented policies, citations, numbers | Grounding, citations, abstention, factuality evals |
| Bias and fairness | Different quality or tone by name, dialect or group | Slice evals by group; counterfactual tests (swap names) |
| Transparency | Users don't know they're talking to AI or why it said something | Disclose AI use; show sources; explain limits |
| Accountability | Nobody owns a bad outcome | Named owner per system; incident process |
| Human oversight | Automated decisions with no review or appeal | Approval gates, override paths, appeals |

A counterfactual fairness check is cheap to automate:

## Document the system

Write a short document for every AI feature that matters, often called a system card or model card for your application:

- **Intended use** and users, and uses that are explicitly out of scope.
- **Known limitations:** languages, domains, input types where quality drops.
- **Risks** from your threat model and how each is mitigated.
- **Evaluations:** what you measured, on what data, with what results, including slices by group.
- **Human controls:** where people approve, review, override or appeal.
- **Data:** what goes to which provider, retention, residency.

Keep it in the repo next to the code and update it when the system changes. It doubles as onboarding material and as the first thing an auditor will ask for.

## Regulation, in practice

**EU AI Act.** In force since August 2024, with obligations applying in phases over the following years. It sorts systems by risk:

- *Prohibited practices* (for example certain manipulative systems and social scoring) were the first rules to apply.
- *High-risk* systems, defined by use case (such as employment, credit, education, access to essential services, and some safety components), carry the heaviest duties: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness. Their application dates have been the subject of later proposals to adjust timing, so check the current schedule.
- *Transparency duties* apply more broadly, for example telling people they're interacting with an AI system and marking AI-generated content in certain cases.
- *General-purpose AI model* providers have their own obligations; if you only build on a provider's model, many of these sit with the provider, but your use case may still be high-risk.

The practical question for you: **does our use case fall into a high-risk category?** A support chatbot usually doesn't; a CV-screening tool usually does.

**India's DPDP Act (2023).** A general data protection law rather than an AI law, but it applies whenever your AI system processes digital personal data of people in India. Key ideas: a lawful basis (mostly consent, plus certain legitimate uses), purpose limitation, notice to users, rights to access, correction and erasure, security safeguards and breach reporting, and extra duties for children's data and for entities designated as significant data fiduciaries. Implementing rules were notified in 2025 with phased commencement, so check which provisions are in effect.

Similar threads run through GDPR, US state laws and sector rules (finance, health). The recurring engineering asks are the same: know what data you process and why, let people exercise their rights, keep records, and keep a human able to intervene.

## Auditability

If someone asks "why did the system say this to this customer on 3 March?", you should be able to answer. Record per request:

- model id and version, prompt template version, tool and retrieval config version;
- retrieved document ids and tool calls (ids, not necessarily full text, given privacy);
- guardrail decisions and approvals, with who approved;
- the eval results that were current when that version shipped.

This is the same versioning discipline as Level 7 eval suites, applied to production.

## Check yourself

1. What is a counterfactual fairness test?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Running the same input with only a sensitive attribute changed (such as a name suggesting a different gender or ethnicity) and checking whether outputs differ more than normal run-to-run noise.
</details>

2. Why does the EU AI Act care about use case rather than which model you use?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Risk comes mostly from what decisions the system affects. The same model is low-risk writing marketing copy and high-risk screening job applicants.
</details>

3. Name three things to log so a single answer can be audited later.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Model and version, prompt template version, retrieved document ids, tool calls, guardrail decisions, and approvals with approver identity.
</details>
