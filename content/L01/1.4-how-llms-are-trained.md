# How LLMs are trained

A freshly pretrained model can write convincing prose about almost anything, yet ask it a question and it may reply
with three more questions. The gap between "knows a lot" and "is a useful assistant" is closed by a second phase of
training. Knowing both phases explains much of the behaviour you will fight with or rely on.

## Phase 1: pretraining

The model is trained on a vast text collection (web pages, books, code, papers; trillions of tokens) to do one thing:
predict the next token. No human labels are needed, because the text supplies its own answers. For every position, the
"correct" next token is simply the one that follows in the document. This is **self-supervised learning**.

```
document: "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level."

training examples hidden inside it:
  "Water"                         → " boils"
  "Water boils"                   → " at"
  "Water boils at 100 degrees"    → " Celsius"
  ...
```

The model guesses, the guess is compared with the real token, and the weights are nudged to make the real token a bit
more likely. Repeat trillions of times on thousands of GPUs over weeks or months.

### What it picks up

To predict well across that much text the model has to learn:

- grammar and style in many languages,
- facts that appear often ("Paris" after "capital of France"),
- code syntax and common library usage,
- relationships and reasoning patterns (if a document sets up a proof or a bug fix, the next steps follow a pattern).

### Memorisation vs generalisation

Some text is memorised nearly verbatim, especially things repeated many times (licences, famous quotes). But mostly the
model **generalises**: it learns patterns it can apply to inputs it has never seen. That is why it can answer a question
phrased in a way no document ever used. It is also why it can produce a confident answer about a fact it saw once or
never: the pattern of "an answer goes here" is strong even when the specific knowledge is weak.

## Phase 2: post-training

A pretrained (**base**) model continues documents. Give it "What is the capital of Peru?" and it may continue with more
quiz questions, because that is what such lines are followed by on the web. Post-training reshapes it into an assistant.

| Stage | What happens | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) | Train on curated prompt-and-ideal-response pairs, often tens of thousands to millions | Learns the chat format and to follow instructions |
| Preference training (RLHF, RLAIF, DPO and relatives) | Humans or a model rank pairs of responses; the model is pushed towards preferred ones | More helpful, better formatted, better calibrated tone |
| Reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks | Reward the model when maths answers check out or code passes tests | Stronger reasoning (see 1.7) |
| Safety tuning | Train to decline harmful requests and handle sensitive topics carefully | Refusals and hedges |

**RLHF** (reinforcement learning from human feedback) trains a reward model on human rankings, then optimises the LLM
against it. **RLAIF** replaces some human judges with an AI judge following written principles. **DPO** and similar
methods skip the separate reward model and learn from preference pairs directly. The details vary by lab and change
often; the shape (pretrain, then teach behaviour) is stable.

## Why assistants behave the way they do

Most chat-model habits trace back to post-training:

- **Follows instructions and answers directly**: SFT data showed that pattern.
- **Markdown headings and bullet points everywhere**: raters often preferred structured answers.
- **Refuses or hedges**: safety and preference data rewarded caution, sometimes too much.
- **Asks clarifying questions**: some training rewards it when the request is ambiguous.
- **Agrees with you too readily** (sycophancy): raters tend to prefer answers that agree with them.

When a model's style annoys you, remember it was trained into it, and you can usually steer it back with instructions.

## Try it

Using a provider or Hugging Face, send the same prompt to a base model and to its instruction-tuned sibling (for example a
pair such as `Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B` and `Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct`). Prompt: `List three uses of baking soda.`

```python
from transformers import pipeline

for name in ["Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B", "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct"]:
    gen = pipeline("text-generation", model=name)
    out = gen("List three uses of baking soda.", max_new_tokens=60, do_sample=False)
    print(name, "→", out[0]["generated_text"])
```

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

The base model typically continues the text as if it were part of a document: repeating the line, adding similar list
prompts, or rambling into an unrelated paragraph. The instruct model gives a numbered list of three uses. (For a fair
comparison, the instruct model should be given the prompt through its chat template, which pipelines do automatically
when you pass a list of messages.) Both share the same pretrained knowledge; only post-training differs.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why does pretraining not need human-labelled data?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The target at every position is the next token in the real text, so the text labels itself. That is self-supervision.
</details>

2. A base model knows that Lima is Peru's capital. Why might it still fail to answer "What is the capital of Peru?"

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It was only trained to continue documents, not to respond to questions. The likely continuation of a question line may
be more questions. Post-training teaches the question-answer behaviour.
</details>

3. Name one assistant behaviour that comes from preference training and one risk it creates.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Well-structured, polite, helpful-sounding answers. The risk is sycophancy or over-confidence: raters can reward answers
that sound good or agree with them over answers that are correct.
</details>
