# Benchmarks and leaderboards

Every model launch comes with a table of scores, and every week a leaderboard changes hands. These numbers are useful,
just not for the decision people usually use them for. Learn to read them as a filter, not a verdict.

## What a benchmark is

A benchmark is a **fixed set of tasks with a fixed scoring rule**, so different models can be measured the same way.
For example: 500 maths problems, scored by exact match on the final number. The fixedness is the strength (fair
comparison) and the weakness (it measures only those tasks, in that format).

## Names you'll see on model cards

| Benchmark (examples) | Roughly measures | Note |
|---|---|---|
| MMLU, MMLU-Pro | Broad knowledge across dozens of subjects, multiple choice | Original MMLU is near saturation for top models |
| GPQA (Diamond) | Graduate-level science questions designed to be hard to look up | Small set, so a few points are noise |
| SWE-bench (Verified) | Fixing real GitHub issues so that tests pass | Closer to real coding work; harness matters a lot |
| LiveCodeBench and similar | Recent competitive programming problems | Refreshed to limit contamination |
| AIME, MATH | Competition maths | Strongly favours reasoning models |
| Humanity's Last Exam | Very hard expert questions across fields | Designed to stay unsaturated for longer |
| Long-context tests (e.g. RULER) | Using information spread across long inputs | Shows the gap between advertised and usable context |

New benchmarks appear constantly as old ones saturate; the categories (knowledge, reasoning, coding, maths, long
context, agentic tasks) are what to remember.

## Kinds of leaderboard

- **Human-preference arenas** (LMArena, formerly Chatbot Arena): people compare two anonymous answers and vote; votes
  become an Elo-style rating. Good signal for general chat appeal; influenced by style, length and formatting.
- **Aggregated dashboards** (for example Artificial Analysis): combine benchmark scores with measured price, speed and
  latency across providers. Good for the cost-quality-speed picture.
- **Open-model leaderboards** (on Hugging Face and elsewhere): compare open weights under a common evaluation harness.

## Why rankings don't transfer to you

- **Different tasks**: a top maths score says little about classifying your insurance claims.
- **Different prompts and formats**: benchmarks use specific prompts; your app uses different ones.
- **Averages hide variance**: a model can lead on average and be worst on the category you care about.
- **Someone else's definition of good**: arena voters reward pleasant, detailed answers; you may want terse JSON.

## What inflates published scores

- **Contamination**: test questions leaked into training data, so the model partly remembers answers.
- **Saturation**: when everyone scores 90%+, differences are mostly noise.
- **Overfitting to public sets**: models tuned specifically to do well on famous benchmarks.
- **Favourable settings**: many samples with the best one counted, special prompts, extra reasoning budget, or a
  custom harness, sometimes noted only in a footnote.

Always read the footnotes, and be wary of comparisons across tables from different sources.

## From leaderboard to shortlist

Use public numbers for the one job they do well: cutting a field of fifty models down to three to five. Then decide
with your own examples.

```python
shortlist = ["model-a", "model-b", "model-c"]
examples = load_jsonl("my_examples.jsonl")      # 20-50 real inputs with expected outputs

for m in shortlist:
    results = [grade(call(m, ex["input"]), ex["expected"]) for ex in examples]
    print(m, f"accuracy={sum(results)/len(results):.0%}")
```

It is common for the model ranked third publicly to win on your data, or for two models to tie and the cheaper one to win.

## Try it

Pick a task you care about (for example, extracting action items from meeting notes). Use a leaderboard to shortlist
three models. Write ten real examples with expected outputs, run all three, and compare your ranking with the
leaderboard's.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Typical outcome: the gap between models on your ten examples is smaller than the leaderboard suggests, and the ranking
often differs. You'll also notice things no leaderboard captures: one model adds unwanted commentary, another misses
implied action items, a third is twice as fast. Ten examples is too few for confident conclusions; treat it as a first
signal and grow the set to 50+ before a final choice. The exercise shows the leaderboard did its job (none of the three
was bad) but couldn't make the final call.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. What is benchmark contamination and why does it matter?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Test questions (or close variants) appeared in the model's training data, so a high score may reflect memory rather than
ability. The score overstates how the model will perform on new inputs like yours.
</details>

2. Model X tops a human-preference arena. Why might it still be the wrong choice for a data-extraction pipeline?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Arena votes reward general chat quality, style and helpful-sounding detail. Extraction needs precise, terse, correctly
formatted output; a different model may be more accurate, faster and cheaper on that task.
</details>

3. What is the right use of a leaderboard in model selection?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

To narrow many candidates to a short list, which you then evaluate on your own examples for quality, latency and cost.
</details>
