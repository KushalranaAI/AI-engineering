# Strengths and failure modes

LLMs are excellent at some things and quietly unreliable at others, and the two often look identical from the outside:
a fluent, confident paragraph. Your job is to know in advance which is which, so you put safeguards where they are
needed and don't waste them where they aren't.

## What they do well

| Strength | Example |
|---|---|
| Transforming text | Rewrite a support reply in a friendlier tone; convert notes to a formatted email |
| Summarising | Condense a 20-page contract into key obligations |
| Extracting | Pull name, date, amount and invoice number into JSON |
| Classifying | Route tickets to billing, technical or account |
| Understanding messy input | Make sense of typo-filled, half-structured user text |
| Code | Write, explain, refactor and review code |
| Translation | Across major languages, with tone preserved |
| Brainstorming | Ten headline options, edge cases you missed |
| Learning from a few examples | Follow a new format from two or three samples |

Common thread: tasks where the answer is mostly *in the input* or follows *widely seen patterns*.

## How they fail

| Failure | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Hallucination | Invented citation, API method, statistic or policy that sounds right |
| Arithmetic slips | Wrong totals in long calculations |
| Brittle reasoning | Correct on one phrasing, wrong when numbers or names change |
| Ignoring instructions | Breaks a length limit or format, especially with many rules |
| Prompt sensitivity | Small wording changes swing the output |
| Context problems | Misses a fact buried in a long input |
| Stale knowledge | Confidently describes last year's pricing or a deprecated library |
| Nondeterminism | Different answers across runs |
| Overconfidence | Wrong answers stated with the same certainty as right ones |

## Why hallucination happens

The model is trained to produce *likely* text, then tuned to produce *preferred* text. Neither step directly rewards
saying "I don't know". When asked for a fact it saw rarely or never, the pattern "a specific answer goes here" is still
strong, so it produces one. There is no internal fact-check that runs before a token is emitted. Fluency and accuracy are
separate properties, and training optimised mostly for the first.

Practical mitigations come later in the path: give the facts in context (RAG), let the model call tools for lookups and
arithmetic, require citations, validate outputs in code, and evaluate systematically. A prompt telling the model "don't
hallucinate" helps a little at best.

## The better question

"Can the model do X?" is almost always yes, for some inputs, some of the time. The engineering question is:

> **How reliably does this model do X, on inputs like mine, under the conditions of my app?**

That means a test set, a pass rate and a decision about the failure rate you can live with:

```python
cases = [
    {"q": "Extract the total from: 'Subtotal $40, tax $3.20, total $43.20'", "expect": "43.20"},
    {"q": "Extract the total from: 'Amount due: EUR 1.250,00'",                "expect": "1250.00"},
    # ... 50 more drawn from real inputs, including awkward ones
]
passed = sum(run_model(c["q"]).strip() == c["expect"] for c in cases)
print(f"{passed}/{len(cases)} = {passed/len(cases):.0%}")
```

Ninety-five per cent might be fine for drafting replies a human reviews, and unacceptable for posting payments
automatically. The same model can be the right choice for one and wrong for the other.

## Try it

Write 10 questions in four groups and ask a model each one, noting its answer and how confident it sounds:
(a) 3 well-known facts, (b) 3 obscure facts (a small town's founding year, a niche paper's third author), (c) 2 questions
with a false premise ("Why did Einstein win the 1930 Nobel Prize in Chemistry?"), (d) 2 questions without enough
information ("What time does my flight leave?").

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Typical results: (a) correct and confident. (b) a mix of correct answers and plausible fabrications, often with no change
in tone; that is the dangerous case. (c) newer models often catch the false premise, but some will invent an explanation.
(d) most models will say they lack the information, since post-training targets this case. The key observation is that
confidence of tone is a poor signal of correctness, especially in group (b). Safeguards are needed exactly where the
model's knowledge is thin.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why is a hallucinated answer often as fluent and confident as a correct one?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The model generates likely-sounding text token by token and has no built-in verification step. Fluency comes from
language patterns, which are strong whether or not the specific fact is known.
</details>

2. Name two tasks that suit an LLM well with minimal safeguards and one that needs strong safeguards.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Well-suited: rewriting text in a new tone, summarising a provided document, classifying short messages. Needs
safeguards: anything relying on precise recalled facts or arithmetic with consequences, such as quoting legal citations
or computing payment amounts.
</details>

3. Rephrase "Can GPT summarise our contracts?" into the question an engineer should ask.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"On a representative sample of our contracts, how often does this model's summary capture every key obligation without
adding wrong ones, and is that rate good enough for how the summaries will be used?"
</details>
