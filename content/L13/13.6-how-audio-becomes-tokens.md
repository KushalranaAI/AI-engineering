# How audio becomes tokens

Audio is the modality where engineering choices bite hardest: it is billed by time, judged on latency, and full of
information that disappears the moment you turn it into text. Before building anything with speech, it helps to know
what the model actually receives.

## Waveform to tokens

Sound arrives as a **waveform**: amplitude samples, typically 16,000 per second for speech models (16 kHz), often
more from a microphone. Models rarely read raw samples. The usual path:

```
waveform (16,000 samples/s)
  → short overlapping windows (e.g. 25 ms every 10 ms)
  → log-Mel spectrogram: energy per frequency band per window (e.g. 80 bands × 100 frames/s)
  → audio encoder (convolutions + transformer) → audio embeddings
  → projected into the LLM's token space
```

A **spectrogram** is a picture of sound: time on one axis, frequency on the other, colour for loudness. That is why
speech encoders look a lot like vision encoders. Newer audio-native models may instead use a **neural audio codec**
that turns sound into discrete tokens, which also lets them generate audio by predicting those tokens.

Whisper, for example, takes 30-second windows of 16 kHz audio as an 80-band log-Mel spectrogram (newer versions use
128 bands) and encodes them before its decoder writes text.

## Billed by the second

Text cost follows the number of words. Audio cost follows **duration**:

```
one provider's example scheme: ~32 tokens per second of audio
60-minute call   → 3,600 s × 32 = 115,200 tokens
same call as text transcript (~150 words/min × 60 × ~1.3 tokens/word) ≈ 11,700 tokens
```

Silence, hold music and background chatter are paid for exactly like speech. Trimming silence with voice activity
detection before sending audio is often the single biggest cost saving. Speech-to-text services are usually priced
per audio minute instead of per token, which hides the same reality.

## Transcribe first or audio-native

| | Transcribe, then prompt | Audio-native model |
|---|---|---|
| Pipeline | STT model → text → any LLM | Audio straight into a multimodal model |
| Cost | Cheap STT + short text prompt | Many audio tokens per minute |
| Debuggable | Yes: read the transcript | Harder |
| Choice of LLM | Any text model | Only models with audio input |
| Hears tone, emphasis, overlap | No | Yes, to a degree |

For "summarise this meeting" or "extract the order details", transcribe-then-prompt is usually cheaper and easier to
inspect. For "was the customer upset?", "who interrupted whom?" or pronunciation coaching, you need the audio.

## What transcripts drop

A transcript keeps words and loses:

- **Tone and emotion**: "fine" said warmly vs flatly.
- **Emphasis**: "I didn't say *he* took it" vs "I didn't say he *took* it".
- **Hesitation and pace**: pauses, fillers and speed, which signal uncertainty.
- **Overlap**: two people talking at once usually becomes one garbled line.
- **Non-speech sound**: laughter, a door slam, a baby crying.

Some STT systems add tags or timestamps for a few of these, but most of the signal is gone.

## Latency matters more

A document summary that takes eight seconds is fine. A voice reply that takes eight seconds feels broken. Audio
pipelines are judged in hundreds of milliseconds, which pushes you towards streaming transcription, smaller models,
and overlapping steps (covered in the voice agents lesson of this level).

## Where recognition breaks

- **Accents** under-represented in training data.
- **Code-switching**: speakers moving between languages mid-sentence, such as Hindi and English.
- **Domain jargon**: drug names, product codes, internal acronyms.
- **Poor microphones** and far-field audio (a phone on a table).
- **Long silences**, which some models fill with hallucinated text.

Measure these on your own recordings before choosing a model.

## Try it

Take a 30-second clip of someone speaking with clear emotion. Run it through a speech-to-text model and give the
transcript to a text LLM, then send the raw audio to an audio-capable model. Ask both: "What was the speaker's mood,
and what in the audio tells you?"

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

The text pipeline can only infer mood from word choice, so it often says "neutral" or guesses from vocabulary. The
audio-native model can cite pace, pitch, sighs or laughter. Compare token usage too: the audio request will cost more
for 30 seconds than the transcript-based one.
</details>

## Check yourself

1. Why does trimming silence reduce the cost of an audio request?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Audio is tokenised (and billed) per second of duration regardless of content, so removing silent stretches removes
tokens without losing speech.
</details>

2. A team wants to extract action items from recorded meetings. Pipeline or audio-native, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Usually a transcription pipeline: action items live in the words, transcripts are cheap, readable and work with any
text LLM, and you can keep timestamps for reference. Audio-native adds cost without much benefit here.
</details>

3. Why does a speech encoder resemble a vision encoder?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Audio is commonly converted to a spectrogram, a 2D time-frequency image, and encoded with convolutional and
transformer layers much like image patches.
</details>
